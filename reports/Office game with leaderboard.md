# Lantern Island Makes Helping the Winning Move

Yes. SVaaN can design an office game that is much better than Wolf and Treasure, and the research says which way to push it. The strongest workplace evidence shows that games at work help only modestly, and only when people freely consent to them. Narrative and teamwork carry most of the benefit, rewarding raw work output backfires, and a public board that ranks recognition *received* reduces helping, while a board that ranks recognition *given* increases it. Wolf and Treasure has good bones: an island story, evidence with reliability levels, a non-elimination treasure mode, and a modern stack. Its headline mode, though, eliminates colleagues and rewards lying to them. It also splits a small player base across two separate games and has no leadership board, no link to real work, and no learning loop. The proposal is **Lantern Island**, a single cooperative, seasonal mystery. Mixed squads called Packs, each combining staff with interns and office with remote players, solve a three-minute daily case and weekly puzzles written by colleagues. Players also give one another a small weekly allowance of "Lanterns" for mentoring, unblocking, initiative and bridging, and those Lanterns literally light the island map. Six-week seasons end in a live hybrid "Unmasking" and a "Lantern Keepers" ceremony. The Leadership Board ranks Packs publicly, shows individuals only as a top five plus their own neighbourhood, has a separate Rookie division, and ranks *giving* rather than popularity. The six interns can ship a playable Season Zero in about four of their twelve planned sprints. The design stays inside India's Online Gaming Act (free to play, no stakes), is ready for the DPDP Act's May 2027 obligations (opt-in, minimal data), and keeps prizes non-cash and under the ₹15,000 tax-free gift limit. The main caveat is that most of the evidence comes from classrooms and labs, not Indian offices, so SVaaN should run the game as a measured pilot and judge it at weeks 8–12, not in the first month.

**How to read this report.** The first section reports research findings, each with an inline citation. Sections two through six are design proposals: they use the research but are SVaaN-specific judgements, and none of them has been tested as a package. Where a design choice rests directly on a finding, the citation sits next to it. Where it does not, the text says so.

## The evidence: games help modestly, and only when people opt in

### Gamification works, but points and leaderboards alone are its weakest form

The best meta-analysis of gamified learning finds real but modest effects: **g = 0.49 on cognitive outcomes and g = 0.36 on motivation**. Only the cognitive effect held up in the most rigorous studies. **Game fiction (narrative) and social interaction** were the significant moderators of behavioural outcomes ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). A review of **819 studies** found results that "lean towards positive" but noted that "the amount of mixed results is remarkable" ([Koivisto & Hamari 2019](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/39369087?lang=en_US)). In a controlled experiment, specific elements had specific effects. Badges, leaderboards and performance graphs raised feelings of competence. Avatars, stories and teammates raised social relatedness. Nothing raised perceived autonomy, and the authors concluded that gamification "is not effective per se" ([Sailer et al. 2017](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Sailer-et-al.-(2017))). Gartner's 2012 forecast that **80% of gamified apps would fail** blamed organisations that reach for "points, badges and leader boards" rather than real game design ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2012/11/27/badges-beware-80-of-gamification-apps-will-end-up-being-losers-says-gartner)). Taken together, a story plus teams is the evidence-backed core, and XP and tokens are garnish.

### Consent decides whether a workplace game helps or hurts

The most rigorous workplace study is a field experiment with 200+ salespeople at a tech company. The game **raised positive affect among employees who consented and lowered it among those who did not**. It produced no sales gain, and non-consenters' performance dipped slightly. A follow-up lab study found that consent comes from **legitimacy** (the game feels fair and appropriate) and **personal agency** ([Mollick & Rothbard, SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2277103); [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/gamification-powering-game/)). The well-known failures all share the opposite features: imposed participation, public individual rankings on speed, opaque scoring, and ties to monitoring. Disneyland's laundry leaderboard was nicknamed the "electronic whip" and bred "competition and dissension" ([Consumerist](https://consumerist.com/2011/10/21/disneyland-hotel-workers-face-electronic-whip/)). Critics of Amazon's warehouse games argued that they masked rising productivity targets ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2019/05/22/amazon-warehouse-games/)). A qualitative study of two large firms found that the main risk is a game being used, or merely *seen* as used, for appraisal or pressure ([Bournemouth/core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/160605944.pdf)).

### Rewarding output crowds out motivation and invites gaming

A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected, contingent tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, with **d between −0.28 and −0.40** ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf)). A 40-year meta-analysis (N = 212,468) found that intrinsic motivation predicts **quality** and incentives predict **quantity**. When incentives were tied directly to performance, intrinsic motivation mattered less ([Cerasoli, Nicklin & Ford 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24491020/)). For a software company, where quality is the product, that is a strong argument against scoring commits or tickets. In a 16-week course built on mandatory badges and a leaderboard, students ended with **lower intrinsic motivation and lower exam scores** than a control group ([Hanus & Fox 2015](https://dblp.org/rec/journals/ce/HanusF15)). Product history shows that counters reshape behaviour whether or not that is healthy. Duolingo rebalanced XP after its leagues inflated low-value time ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well)). After GitHub removed its streak counter, long streaks became less common ([Moldon et al., arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.02371)).

### Leaderboards act as hard goals, and recognition boards must rank giving

Without any instruction, people treat a leaderboard as a "difficult goal" and aim for the top. That only motivates those who believe the top is reachable ([Landers, Bauer & Callan](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/gamification-of-task-performance-with-leaderboards-a-goal-setting/)). Relative boards, which show people near you, kept all positions equally engaged in one study ([Bai et al. via Na & Han 2023](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/INTR-12-2021-0897)). Researchers also recommend limiting the public display of bottom ranks ([HKU](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/368255/1/content.pdf)). The single most important finding for SVaaN's must-have comes from peer recognition. **Ranking employees by recognition received reduced proactive helping, while ranking them by recognition given increased it**, apparently because a "received" board makes colleagues question one another's motives ([Evans, Presslee & Vandenberg, *The Accounting Review* 2025](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/peer-to-peer-recognition-leaderboards-and-employee-proactive-helping-behavior)). A second experiment found that publicly disclosed peer recognition can reduce later helping ([Wang, Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/ctr-sustainability-performance-management/sites/default/files/uploads/files/for_sendout_-_p2p_backfire_-_pei.pdf)). Exclusivity can still help. In a field experiment, unannounced thank-you cards given only to the **top 3 of 8** workers raised later performance, and the gain came mostly from the *non-recipients* ([Bradler et al.](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/39189/2013-038.pdf)). Well-run products cap the cheapest signals. Stack Overflow limits vote reputation to **200 per day** and reverses serial voting ([Stack Overflow](https://support.stackenterprise.co/support/solutions/articles/22000294430-reputation-and-voting); [serial voting](https://stackoverflowteams.com/help/serial-voting-reversed)). HeyTaco gives each user **5 expiring tacos a day**, reasoning that more would dilute each one ([HeyTaco](https://heytaco.com/docs/article/78-can-i-adjust-daily-taco-limits)). Zappos barred managers from giving peer bonuses to their direct reports ([UN HR compilation](https://hr.un.org/sites/hr.un.org/files/Private%20Sector%20Recognition%20and%20Rewards%20A%20Collection%20of%20Examples.pdf)). Strava's "Local Legend" rewards the most consistent participant over a rolling 90 days and shows a histogram instead of a ranked list ([DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2020/06/strava-legends-feature.html)). Leadership itself can be made observable. Google's Project Oxygen distilled great management into behaviours such as coaching, empowering, communicating and caring about career development ([Google re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/following-the-data-the-research-behind-great-managers)). Kouzes and Posner's Five Practices include "Enable Others to Act", "Challenge the Process" and "Encourage the Heart" ([Boise State](https://www.boisestate.edu/academics-deptchairs/home/resources-for-academic-leaders/new-academic-leaders-program/the-5-practices-of-exemplary-leadership/)). Interns can show every one of these behaviours, not just managers.

### Cooperative play and psychological safety drive team gains

Team building has a **moderate positive effect**, strongest on trust and coordination ([Klein et al. 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1046496408328821)). An older meta-analysis found **role clarification** to be the component most linked to performance ([Salas et al. 1999](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/104649649903000303)). In an experiment with 80 teams of strangers, **45 minutes of cooperative video gaming raised subsequent team task scores by about 20%**, more than goal-setting training did ([BYU](https://news.byu.edu/news/study-collaborative-video-games-could-increase-office-productivity)). The teams were strangers, though, and teams who already know each other may react differently. Google's Project Aristotle ranked **psychological safety** first among team-effectiveness factors and found that colocation did *not* matter ([Google re:Work](https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/understanding-team-effectiveness)). That supports a hybrid design that treats remote players as full participants. Team-based recognition at six fast-food franchises lifted engagement, effort and sales growth ([IRF summary of Presslee et al.](https://theirf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2024_09_IRF-Academic_Research_In_Action.pdf)). Novelty fades on a known schedule. In a 14-week study of 756 students, the effect began to decline after **about four weeks** and recovered between weeks 6 and 10 ([Rodrigues et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)). Proximity bias, the habit of favouring people who are physically present, is widely documented in practitioner sources, and the standard fixes are everyone on their own device, rotating times, and async channels ([UC Davis](https://gsm.ucdavis.edu/news/what-proximity-bias); [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/loom/proximity-bias); [Toptal](https://www.toptal.com/insights/future-of-work/distance-bias/)).

### India: high hierarchy, under-recognised juniors and three binding rules

India scores **77 on Hofstede's Power Distance** index ([arXiv table](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.2741)). GLOBE places the Southern Asia cluster high on **in-group collectivism** ([GLOBE](https://globeproject.com/results/clusters/southern-asia%3Fmenu=list.html)). Upward communication is "culturally discouraged" ([Subhakaran & Dyaram](https://www.emeraldinsight.com/doi/abs/10.1108/IJPPM-10-2017-0276?af=R)). Commentary from SHRM India adds that face-saving discourages admitting ignorance ([SHRM India](https://www.shrm.org/in/topics-tools/news/blogs/why-psychological-safety-in-india-requires-more-than-an-open-door-policy)). Recognition is what people want, and juniors get the least of it. **92% of Indian professionals** call recognition their strongest motivator ([YourStory on Avtar/EY](https://yourstory.com/socialstory/2025/11/recognition-at-workplace-strongest-motivation-factor-study-report)). Only **55% feel truly recognised**, and new hires and non-managers are "significantly under-recognised" ([GPTW India × Vantage Circle](https://www.greatplacetowork.in/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/the-recognition-effect-great-place-to-work-x-vantage-circle-1.pdf)). **63%** rate recognition from senior leaders as critical ([CXOToday on Indeed](https://cxotoday.com/media-coverage/indian-employees-crave-recognition-and-openness-at-work-indeed-finds/)). Gallup puts engaged employees in India at **23%**, a four-year low ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/713372/rebuilding-indian-workplaces-for-the-future-report.aspx)). Indian IT firms have tried similar ideas. Wipro's TopGear frames real projects as challenges ([People Matters](https://www.peoplematters.in/?p=10430)), and TCS used gamification to break down hierarchical barriers ([HRD Connect](https://www.hrdconnect.com/?p=59999)), though their published results are self-reported. Students and companies prefer internships of four to six months ([Careers360](https://news.careers360.com/most-students-corporates-prefer-4-6-month-internships-remote-work-college-placements-hirepro-report/amp)), so a six-week season fits inside one intern cycle.

Three legal constraints shape the design. The table summarises them as of 24 November 2026. It draws on law-firm commentary rather than the gazette text and is not legal advice.

The evidence has clear limits. Almost all effect sizes come from education. No study tests seasonal resets at work. No Indian study measures comfort with public ranking. Evans et al. is mainly lab-based, and its field trial is still under way. Corporate case figures such as Deloitte, LiveOps and Constellation come from vendors. The designs below are built to fit this evidence, but they are not proven by it.

## Wolf and Treasure has the right island but the wrong villain

*Diagnosis and design judgement, grounded in the findings above.*

The Classic Werewolf mode is the main problem. Social deduction rests on deception and elimination. Even players who enjoy it report that it "leaves many players reeling with anger and frustration" ([Medium/Josh Kerr, anecdotal](https://medium.com/josh-kerr/werewolf-324ed391b6e4)), and that players "feel like they lose if they die, even if the werewolf team wins" ([EconLib](https://www.econlib.org/?p=15285)). In an office, the deception is aimed at real colleagues. That directly cuts against psychological safety, which is the strongest team-effectiveness lever in the evidence. It is riskier still in a high-power-distance setting, where an intern would have to accuse a senior publicly, or be accused by one. Werewolf also favours confident talkers and needs a synchronous quorum, so remote players on patchy connections and quieter interns lose out. Ghost Mode is a sensible patch, but it treats a symptom. Blood on the Clocktower's approach (the dead keep talking and keep one vote) shows that elimination can be softened ([BotC Wiki](https://wiki.bloodontheclocktower.com/Rules_Explanation)). The better move for an office is to have no elimination at all.

The structure is the second problem. Two separate modes split a small player base, staff plus about seven interns, into two half-populated games, and they double the build and content load on six part-time builders. The Treasure Mystery mode's "trust/betrayal" mechanic brings the Werewolf problem back in through the side door. XP and tokens with no purpose beyond the game are the points-badges-leaderboards pattern Gartner warned about. The plan has no Leadership Board, which is the one must-have. It has nothing that connects to real work or learning, and no cadence designed around the four-week novelty dip. The twelve-sprint MVP lands the first playable season late, when the cheapest test would be to put a simple version in front of players early.

A great deal is worth keeping, and the redesign reuses most of the team's work:

| Keep and repurpose | Cut or demote |
|---|---|
| **Lost Island map with 8 zones**: becomes the shared progress map that Lanterns light up | **Player elimination**, all of it |
| **Evidence with reliability levels**: becomes the heart of the weekly deduction and the finale | **Deception aimed at colleagues**: the villain becomes a fictional NPC, the "Shadow" |
| **Puzzles**: become the Case of the Day and colleague-authored Expeditions | **Trust/betrayal between players**: replaced by interdependence (each person holds a fragment) |
| **Secret missions**: become Case Partner missions that connect people across teams | **Two parallel modes**: merged into one seasonal game |
| **Narrative framing**: game fiction is an evidence-backed moderator | **Purpose-less XP/tokens**: points reward learning, sharing and helping only |
| **Werewolf voting and role logic**: reused for Pack deduction votes and an optional, opt-in Trickster role | **Classic Werewolf as a core mode**: at most an occasional, off-the-board "Campfire Night" party game, deferred |
| **Next.js/TypeScript/Supabase stack and six owners** | **A 12-sprint path to first play** |

There is a fair argument for keeping Werewolf. People love it. An office "Traitors" game ran for three weeks over Slack while non-players followed along ([City AM, snippet](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2301881)), and some of the team is clearly excited about it. The proposal keeps the appeal (hidden information, deduction, a big reveal) while removing the costs. The suspect is fictional, and the only "lying" allowed is one opt-in Trickster per season, who plants red herrings about the story, never about colleagues.

## Lantern Island: a cooperative mystery where leadership lights the map

*Design proposal.*

### The premise and core loop

The Lost Island has gone dark. A fictional saboteur, **the Shadow**, has hidden the island's treasure and snuffed out the lanterns in all eight zones. Each season, the company works as a set of **Packs** (the wolves are now the good guys) to gather evidence, deduce where the Shadow hid the treasure and who among the island's fictional characters helped, and relight the island. The core loop has four verbs that tie the four goals together: **Solve** (daily and weekly puzzles, for fun and learning), **Share** (author a puzzle about your own domain, which ties in real work), **Help** (give and earn Lanterns for leadership behaviours, which handles recognition), and **Unmask** (the season finale, for bonding). Every Lantern someone gives lights a lamp in the zone tied to its category. The island's overall brightness is therefore a company-wide cooperative score, and if it crosses a threshold by the finale, *everyone* gets the shared reward, such as a team lunch. Helping is never only a private act of competition, because it visibly lights the map that everyone shares.

Evidence cards keep Wolf and Treasure's reliability levels (Solid, Shaky, Rumour). The cards are dealt so that each Pack member holds *different* fragments. As in Hanabi, where players see teammates' cards but not their own and must coordinate through limited hints ([Bard et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1902.00506)), a Pack can only solve the case by pooling what each member holds. Some fragments come from physical clues in the office, such as a QR poster, and their decoder keys go only to remote members. The design forces office and remote players to depend on each other instead of competing.

### Cadence: three minutes a day, one big night per season

| Layer | Time and format | What happens | Evidence it leans on |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Daily: Case of the Day** | About 3 min, async, any time 9am–7pm | One puzzle, the same for everyone, released each morning. Rotating types: company and product trivia, a spot-the-bug snippet, a cipher, a diagram puzzle. Solving yields one evidence fragment for your Pack. The result is a shareable, spoiler-free grid. | Wordle's one-a-day scarcity and shared puzzle ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2256733)) |
| **Weekly: Expedition** | 20–30 min within a 48h window | A puzzle authored by a colleague about their real domain, released Monday. Also a **Pack deduction vote** (async, 48h) on one question in the mystery, and a weekly **Case Partner** pairing across Packs. | Colleague-authored content and Donut-style pairing (design inference); escape-room team puzzles ([PMC review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11290095/)) |
| **Mid-season twist** | Week 3–4 | The Shadow "strikes": one zone goes dark, a new puzzle type appears, and Packs get a chance to catch up. | Novelty dip at about week 4 ([Rodrigues et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)) |
| **Season** | 6 weeks | A narrative arc that ends with the treasure found and the Shadow unmasked. Packs are reshuffled each season. Seasons are themed on the calendar: Pongal (verify date: about 15 Jan 2027), Holi, IPL, Onam, Diwali. | 3-week corporate ARG and office Traitors runs ([eLearning Industry](https://elearningindustry.com/alternate-reality-game-examples-using-args-corporate-training)); intern cycles of 4–6 months |
| **Finale: The Unmasking** | 60 min live, hybrid, in working hours | Packs combine their fragments in an escape-room-style final challenge, then a 10-minute debrief on what people learned, then the Leadership Board ceremony. It can be attached to an existing all-hands, hackathon or bug bash. | 45-min cooperative play raised team performance about 20% ([BYU](https://news.byu.edu/news/study-collaborative-video-games-could-increase-office-productivity)) |

The daily layer is deliberately tiny. That respects workloads and makes "I played today" cheap, so opting in feels free rather than like another obligation.

### How hybrid players are equal by construction

The rules make remote and office players structurally equal, not merely tolerated. **Everyone plays on their own device**, including people sitting in the same room during the finale, following Atlassian's practice of giving every person their own link ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/loom/proximity-bias)). **Every scoring action is async** with a window of 24 hours or more, apart from the finale, which is held in working hours at a rotating time. **Every physical clue has a digital twin of equal value**, or is designed to be interdependent: the office finds it, and remote players decode it. **Pack composition is constrained**: every Pack has at least one remote and one office player, and at least one intern and one staff member. **Daily puzzles carry no speed bonus**, because speed rewards whoever is online at 9:00 or sitting next to the author. **No points can be earned after 8pm or on weekends.** That prevents the game from rewarding after-hours activity, which falls hardest on people with caregiving duties, long commutes or safety constraints on late travel; women make up about 36% of India's tech workforce ([Deccan Herald on Nasscom](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/business%2Feconomy%2F36-of-it-workforce-are-women-data-doubles-in-a-decade-2656362)). Each season, the admin checks one fairness number: remote players' share of Lanterns received compared with their share of headcount. The app should be a light, mobile-first PWA, because India is mobile-heavy at about 31 GB per user per month ([Deccan Herald on Nokia](https://www.deccanherald.com/technology/indias-average-monthly-mobile-data-usage-per-user-hits-31gb-in-2025-nokia-3950714)). Reminders go to the team's existing chat, and any WhatsApp nudges should be few, in working hours, and never include rankings, since 91% of online adults in India talk to businesses on WhatsApp ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/12/whatsapp-brings-meta-verified-customized-messages-to-small-businesses-in-india)) and a ranking posted there is a public ranking.

### How real work and learning feed in, without surveillance

One rule governs this: **the game only knows what players tell it.** It never reads commits, tickets, attendance, chat activity or calendars. Real work enters through four voluntary channels. The first is **Expeditions authored by colleagues**. Each week a different author, ideally a staff member paired with an intern, turns a piece of their real domain into a puzzle: a real (sanitised) bug to spot, a system diagram with a missing component, a customer story with a logic gap. The author earns "Architect" points per distinct solver, and an intern co-author learns the domain by having to explain it. This copies the best-documented corporate success pattern. Microsoft's Language Quality Game produced real value (flagged translation issues) as a voluntary side effect of play ([Microsoft TechNet](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/technet-wiki/9299.language-quality-game)). The second channel is **Learning Quests**. An intern or anyone else posts a request such as "explain our deploy pipeline to me in 30 minutes" or "review my first PR with me". A colleague takes it, and the *requester* confirms completion, which records a Guide act for the helper and learning points for both. Requests can come from anyone, so asking a question becomes a scoring move rather than a loss of face. That speaks directly to the Indian finding that juniors avoid visibly asking ([SHRM India](https://www.shrm.org/in/topics-tools/news/blogs/why-psychological-safety-in-india-requires-more-than-an-open-door-policy)). The third channel is **Case Partner missions**, the Assassin target-chain idea turned into connection. Each week you are secretly paired with someone outside your Pack, and your mission is to learn what they are building well enough to answer one question about it. Your partner confirms. The fourth channel is a small **Glue-Work Board**, a TopGear-style bounty board ([People Matters](https://www.peoplematters.in/?p=10430)) limited to work that usually goes unrewarded: an onboarding doc, a missing test, a knowledge session. Leads post the items voluntarily, the requester confirms completion, the items earn Spark credit, and each person can claim at most two per season. Core sprint deliverables are never on the board, so the game cannot turn into a throughput leaderboard.

## The Leadership Board ranks Packs publicly and rewards giving, not popularity

*Design proposal. This is the must-have, so the board is specified in detail.*

The Leadership Board has two halves on one screen. **Season Standings** is the ranking leaderboard: who is ahead in the game. The **Lantern Board** is the leadership-recognition board: who is mentoring, helping, taking initiative and bridging the office–remote gap. They use different currencies on purpose. Explorer Points (EP) measure play and learning. Lanterns measure leadership behaviour. Neither can be converted into the other, so nobody can grind puzzles to look like a leader, or trade favours to win the game.

### Season Standings: Packs first, individuals in neighbourhoods

The headline ranking is the **Pack table**, shown in full, because team-based recognition lifted engagement in the field ([IRF/Presslee](https://theirf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2024_09_IRF-Academic_Research_In_Action.pdf)) and fits India's in-group collectivism. A Pack's score is the *average* EP of its active members, so a member on leave or sitting exams does not drag their Pack down, plus a deduction-accuracy bonus from the weekly votes and the finale. Individual standings appear in **two divisions**: **Crew** (staff) and **Rookies** (interns, and anyone in their first 90 days). Each division publicly shows only its **top five** plus the viewer's own rank with two places either side, which is the relative "neighbourhood" view supported by the research. **No one's bottom rank is ever shown to anyone else.** New players carry a "provisional" tag until they have played five Cases, borrowing the idea behind Glicko and TrueSkill that early results are uncertain ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/trueskill-ranking-system/)). An "Island Regular" title goes to the people who solved the most Cases in the season, shown Strava-style as a histogram rather than a list. There is no streak counter, because GitHub's experience shows that streaks punish days off.

| Explorer Points source | EP | Cap |
|---|---|---|
| Solve the Case of the Day | 10, +1 to +3 for fewer attempts (no speed bonus) | 1 per day by design |
| Solve the weekly Expedition | 20 | 1 per week |
| Author an approved Expedition (Architect) | 5 per distinct solver | 100 per puzzle. Authors and reviewers cannot solve their own puzzle. |
| Complete a Case Partner mission (partner confirms) | 15 each | 1 per week |
| Complete a Learning Quest (requester confirms) | 20 each | 2 per week per person |
| Correct Pack deduction vote | 10 per Pack member | Weekly |

### The Lantern Board: five leadership lamps, ranked on giving

Every player, from the founder to the newest intern, receives **three Lanterns a week**. They expire on Sunday and do not roll over, following the scarcity logic of HeyTaco and Bonusly. To give one, you pick a recipient, pick one of four behaviour categories, and write at least one sentence about a *specific act* ("walked me through the RLS policy bug on Tuesday"). The fifth category is earned by giving. The categories map onto Project Oxygen and Kouzes–Posner, so they describe real leadership behaviours rather than popularity, and each lights its own zone of the island:

| Lamp (island zone) | Leadership behaviour | Framework anchor | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Guide** (Lighthouse) | Mentoring and coaching | Oxygen "good coach", "career development"; Enable Others | Completed Learning Quests; a pairing session; a review walkthrough |
| **Unblocker** (Bridge of Ropes) | Helping someone get unstuck | Oxygen "interest in team members' success"; Enable Others | Debugging help; answering a question; covering for someone |
| **Spark** (Forge) | Initiative and improvement | Challenge the Process | Glue-Work Board items; proposing or shipping a fix to a process |
| **Bridge** (Harbour) | Connecting people and communicating | Oxygen "good communicator" | Docs, demos, looping remote colleagues in, Expedition authoring |
| **Encourager** (Beacon) | Recognising others | Encourage the Heart | *Earned by giving* specific, high-quality Lanterns to varied people |

What the Lantern Board shows publicly follows the Evans et al. finding: **rank giving, not receiving**. The board has three tiers. The **Lamp badges** for Guide, Unblocker, Spark and Bridge are threshold awards, not ranks. Anyone who receives Lantern Points worth 3 or more from at least three *different* givers in a season earns that lamp, so many people can "win" and nobody is ranked by how much praise they collected. The **Encourager ranking** is the only ranked list on the Lantern Board. It ranks people by the number of quality-passing Lanterns they *gave*, weighted toward giving to different people, and shows the top five per division. At the end of the season come the **Lantern Keepers**. The rotating panel selects up to **three Crew Keepers and one "Rising Lantern" from the Rookies**, choosing only from lamp holders and working from the written evidence. The reasons are published. This deliberately exclusive top-three award follows the finding that exclusive recognition lifted the performance of everyone who did not receive it ([Bradler et al.](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/39189/2013-038.pdf)). Lantern counts received are shown only to the recipient, along with every message they received, which they can save. One part of this is untested: whether *threshold badges* for received recognition avoid the harm Evans et al. found for *ranked* received boards. It is a reasoned compromise, and SVaaN should watch the helping metrics below to check it.

### Anti-gaming rules, written down and published

The rules are published in full before Season 1, because legitimacy is what drives consent ([Mollick & Rothbard](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2277103)). The **pair decay** rule means that within a season, the first Lantern from a given giver to a given recipient counts 1.0 Lantern Point, the second 0.5, and any further ones zero, so friends cannot farm one another. This follows Stack Overflow's serial-voting logic. Under the **reciprocity flag**, A→B and B→A in the same week are both held at 0.5 until a panel member clears them. Under the **quality gate**, a Lantern needs a category plus a concrete act, and the panel can void boilerplate such as "great work!" with a private note to the giver. For **hierarchy neutralisation**, Lanterns from the founder and from managers to their own reports appear on the feed as a highlighted "Founder's Note" or "Lead's Note" but carry **zero Lantern Points**. This adapts Zappos's rule for a company where the founder effectively manages everyone. Seniors' praise stays visible, which matters because 63% of Indian employees value recognition from leaders, but in a high-power-distance office it cannot decide who wins. A **rotating calibration panel** of three, made up of one intern, one non-manager staff member and one lead, with the founder ratifying but not choosing, meets for 30 minutes at the end of each season, and its members cannot win Lantern Keeper that season. **Author and builder exclusions** stop puzzle authors, reviewers, and the interns with production access to answers from scoring on content they can see. Answers are stored hashed. Under the **no-surveillance rule**, nothing outside the game is ingested, ever. Under the **no-appraisal rule**, game scores and Lantern counts are never inputs to appraisals, stipends, pre-placement offers or promotions, and this is written into the consent notice. People can of course cite a Lantern Keeper award in their own self-review if they choose.

### What is public, what is private

| Visible to all players | Visible only to you | Visible only to the panel | Never shown or stored |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full Pack table; top 5 per division; your ±2 neighbourhood; Island Regular histogram | Your EP, exact rank, full history | Reciprocity and quality flags | Anyone's bottom rank |
| Lantern feed (giver, recipient, category, message), with the recipient's opt-in. Each person can hide their entries. | Lanterns you received, with messages and count | Lantern Points received per person, for Keeper selection only | Commits, tickets, attendance, chat or calendar data |
| Lamp badges, Encourager top 5, Lantern Keepers with reasons, island brightness | Your consent settings and display name | — | Any link to appraisal or pay |

Any player can choose a display pseudonym and can leave the public individual boards while staying in their Pack. Non-players can watch the island and the Pack table, as non-players did in the office Traitors game. The founder and admins see aggregate health metrics, not extra individual detail.

### Seasonal reset and the Hall of Fame

At the end of each six-week season, EP, Lantern Points, lamps and divisions all reset, and Packs are reshuffled under the mixing constraints. A reset keeps the top reachable, which goal-setting theory says is what sustains commitment, and gives every intern cohort a fresh start inside its four-to-six-month stay. Permanent recognition lives in a **Hall of Fame**: champion Packs, Lantern Keepers and their published reasons, and the best Expedition authors, all included only with each person's consent. Raw season data is deleted or anonymised within 30 days, which keeps the system ready for DPDP's obligations from May 2027. Interns who move on can export their own Lantern messages as a portfolio. This is the strongest use of the board for a student: a record of specific praise they own, not a score the company holds.

### Rewards that respect the law and the stipend

Every prize is non-cash, symbolic or experiential. Lantern Keepers might get lunch with the founder, the right to choose next season's theme, a conference or course ticket, a signed certificate, or, for interns, a LinkedIn recommendation that quotes their Lantern messages. When the island lights up, the whole company shares a reward such as team lunch, which avoids individual stakes altogether. Nothing is bought, wagered, randomly drawn or convertible to money, which keeps the game a no-stake "social game" under the Act. Track each person's annual total of game prizes plus festival gifts and keep it well below ₹15,000. Remember that a ₹2,000 voucher means far more against an intern's stipend, which averaged about ₹8,000 a month in Internshala's 2024 data ([The People's Board](https://www.thepeoplesboard.com/?p=23771)), than against a staff salary, so status rewards work better than costly ones.

## Six interns can ship Season Zero in four sprints

*Design proposal for the build.*

The aim is to get real players into a cut-down season early, because the novelty curve, fairness and gaming problems only show up with real use. Each owner's existing area maps almost one to one onto the new design:

| Owner (current area) | New ownership in Lantern Island | Season Zero deliverables (sprints 1–4) |
|---|---|---|
| **Kishore** (integration) | Platform, season engine, scoring service | Google sign-in, Supabase schema and row-level security, consent and notice screen, season/week clock and resets, scoring service with caps, pair decay and reciprocity flags, chat notifications, PWA deploy |
| **Kaarthic** (Wolf mode) | **The Shadow mystery engine**: his voting and role logic carries over | Season storyline, suspects and hideouts; weekly evidence drops; async 48h Pack deduction vote; finale Unmasking flow that combines fragments |
| **Ramya** (Treasure mode) | **Island and puzzles** | 8-zone island map with lamp lighting and brightness; Case of the Day player; 3 puzzle types; first 30 Cases written with authors |
| **Sharmitha** (lobby) | **Packs and people** | Pack formation with mixing constraints; profiles, pseudonyms and opt-outs; finale live lobby (own-device); Case Partner pairing (sprint 4 or 5) |
| **Yamini** (evidence/missions) | **Lanterns and quests**: reuses the evidence-reliability and mission model | Lantern giving (3/week, categories, act text), feed with recipient opt-in; Learning Quest request → accept → confirm flow; evidence-card reliability tags |
| **Sindhiya** (shared UI/QA) | **Leadership Board UI, design system, fairness QA** | Pack table, division top-5 plus neighbourhood view, Lantern Board (lamps, Encourager top 5, Keepers), mobile performance budget, an anti-gaming test suite (collusion scripts, pair decay, no-bottom-rank checks), accessibility pass |

The suggested sequence runs as follows. **Sprint 1** delivers sign-in, consent, schema, Packs, and the Case of the Day with hand-written content. **Sprint 2** delivers Lanterns, the feed and caps, plus the island map with lighting and a basic Pack table. **Sprint 3** delivers evidence drops, the deduction vote, Learning Quests, the Rookie division, the neighbourhood view and the full anti-gaming rules. **Sprint 4** delivers the finale lobby and Unmasking, panel tools, season reset with a Hall of Fame stub, and an end-to-end QA pass. Season Zero then runs as a three-week pilot with everyone invited and nobody required. While it runs, sprints 5–8 add the Expedition authoring and review workflow, Case Partner missions, the Glue-Work Board, the mid-season twist content and a small health dashboard. Season 1, with all six weeks, could then launch around Pongal 2027. A cheap step before any code: during sprints 1–2, run the Lantern categories for two weeks on a Google Form posted in team chat. That tests whether people understand Guide, Unblocker, Spark and Bridge, and how many Lanterns they actually give, before the rules are built into code.

**Defer** the following until the core loop has proven itself over two seasons. Classic Werewolf as an optional, unscored "Campfire Night" with BotC-style ghost votes. The opt-in Trickster role. Physical office ARG clues beyond a few QR posters. Festival-specific skins. Avatars and cosmetic shops. Skill ratings such as TrueSkill (unnecessary at this size). The WhatsApp Business API. Any native app. Also defer every idea that touches work-tool data, which ideally means indefinitely. One process rule protects the builders: the interns are also players and rookies, so their build work should be assessed as engineering in their normal evaluations, and nobody with answer access should be scored on that content.

## Risks to watch, and how to tell whether it works

*Design proposal; the risks draw on the research above.*

| Risk | Why it is real | Mitigation built into the design |
|---|---|---|
| **Feels like mandatory fun** | Non-consenters' affect fell in the field ([Mollick & Rothbard](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2277103)) | Opt-in; 3-minute daily core; spectating allowed; rules co-designed with a staff + intern group before Season 1; the founder plays and visibly loses sometimes |
| **Popularity contest or favouritism** | Ranked received recognition reduces helping ([Evans et al.](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/peer-to-peer-recognition-leaderboards-and-employee-proactive-helping-behavior)) | Rank giving only; threshold lamps; pair decay; reciprocity flags; panel with an intern seat; written reasons |
| **Founder or seniors dominate** | High power distance (PDI 77) | Founder's and leads' Notes carry 0 Lantern Points; separate divisions; founder ratifies but does not choose |
| **Interns lose face** | Juniors under-recognised; asking is face-costly | Rookie division; Rising Lantern; no bottom ranks; asking for a Learning Quest earns points |
| **Point farming** | Duolingo and GitHub show counters get gamed | Caps on every source; no speed or volume scoring; authors cannot solve their own puzzles |
| **Remote players sidelined** | Proximity bias ([UC Davis](https://gsm.ucdavis.edu/news/what-proximity-bias)) | Async windows; digital twins; mixed Packs; own-device finale; seasonal remote-share check |
| **Week-4 slump** | Decline after about 4 weeks ([Rodrigues et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)) | Mid-season twist; 6-week seasons; new Packs each season |
| **Drifts into surveillance or appraisal** | Appraisal links are the main enterprise risk ([Bournemouth](https://core.ac.uk/download/160605944.pdf)); DPDP s.7(i) is narrow | Written no-ingestion and no-appraisal rules in the consent notice; deletion at season end |
| **Legal missteps** | PROGA penalties; ₹15,000 cliff; intern status unclear | No stakes ever; non-cash prizes tracked per person; one-time CA/counsel review on interns and data |
| **Build overrun** | Six part-time builders, two former modes | One merged mode; four-sprint Season Zero; long defer list |

Measure the pilot against a baseline taken before Season Zero, and judge it at **weeks 8–12, after a full season, not at launch**, because the first month always flatters a game. The suggested thresholds below are judgement calls for a small team, not benchmarks from the research:

| What to measure | How | Healthy signal (proposed) |
|---|---|---|
| **Voluntary participation** | Share of eligible people opted in; weekly active players in weeks 5–6 vs weeks 1–2 | ≥70% opt in; weeks 5–6 activity ≥60% of weeks 1–2 |
| **Cross-group connection** | Share of Lanterns and Quests crossing staff↔intern and office↔remote lines | ≥50% cross at least one line |
| **Hybrid fairness** | Remote share of Lanterns received ÷ remote share of headcount | Between 0.8 and 1.2 |
| **Helping behaviour** | Learning Quests completed; distinct givers per recipient | Rising across seasons; no recipient depending on one giver |
| **Felt recognition and safety** | A five-item pulse (anonymous) before and after each season: "I feel recognised", "I can ask for help without embarrassment", "I know what colleagues outside my team work on", "the rules are fair", "I'd play next season". Compare with GPTW India's 55% recognised baseline. | Improvement on all five; "rules are fair" ≥75% agree |
| **Learning and work link** | Expeditions authored (and by how many distinct people); quests per intern | Every intern co-authors one Expedition per season |
| **Guardrails** | Opt-outs from public boards, reciprocity flags, voided Lanterns, complaints | Low and stable; any rise triggers a rules review |

Do not use productivity metrics such as tickets closed or commit counts to judge the game. The game should never chase them, and measuring them sends the signal that it does.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the brief. SVaaN asked for a game with a leaderboard, but the evidence says the leaderboard is the most dangerous part of any workplace game and the narrative, the teams and the consent are the safest. So the right answer is not a better leaderboard bolted onto Werewolf. It is a cooperative mystery in which the leadership board *is* the win condition: Lanterns given for mentoring and unblocking literally light the shared map, and the only ranked leadership list rewards generosity rather than popularity. That inversion matters most in India, where hierarchy makes upward praise rare and asking for help costly. A system in which interns score by asking and seniors score by teaching turns both of those cultural frictions into gameplay.

The biggest unknown is whether these mechanics survive contact with SVaaN's actual people. No study has tested threshold badges for received recognition, seasonal resets at work, or ranking in Indian offices, so Lantern Island should be treated as an experiment with published rules and a measured pilot, not a finished product. If Season Zero shows rising cross-group Lanterns and a "rules are fair" score most people agree with, SVaaN will have built something none of the commercial engagement tools in the research offered: a persistent game that recognises leadership behaviour, costs nothing per head, and gives every intern a portfolio of specific praise they keep after they leave.
